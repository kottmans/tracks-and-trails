What to do next, in order

One ordering, and the reason for it. The numbers are a sequence rather than a ranking: each item unblocks or de-risks the one under it. Rewritten 2026-08-06 — the first item used to be the spec that gated five deliverables, and it is written.

  1. — Re-review the withdrawal Before anything new

    The range 7afa295..3578fcc was reviewed and came back Changes requested on five findings. Three are resolved by the ledger no longer existing, and two — the unratified decision and the retained credentials — are resolved by the ruling that produced the withdrawal. The withdrawal itself is unreviewed, and it touches two requirements, three decision entries and the persistence layer. T-150, T-156 and the visible History removal were accepted and need nothing further.

  2. T-107 The format table Foundation

    Named High because REQ-008 and T-109 both act on this table. It also owns proving the phase's first exit criterion — the table matching yt-dlp -F for a fixture set — so it carries a gate as well as a feature. Now the first real deliverable that can start, and the spec it was waiting for exists.

  3. T-113 Resume across a restart Start early

    Placed third despite being startable today, and deliberately not last: it reopens two accepted decisions and carries the phase's highest uncertainty. Discovering what it costs late is how a phase overruns — and it needs no spec, so nothing is lost by learning early.

  4. T-108 Video plus audio, merged After T-107

    Reads the table T-107 builds, and settles the ffmpeg-presence rule that T-109 then shares. Two exit criteria run through here: the merge itself, on both platforms.

  5. T-110 Playlists, properly Structural

    Changes what a job is, so it reaches core/ rather than the UI. Worth taking while the queue is freshly reviewed — T-137 and T-140 each moved part of the playlist story. (T-145 moved History's part of it, and that surface is gone.)

  6. T-109 Post-processing, seven options Largest

    The biggest item in the phase, and the one with a documented history of failing quietly: T-077 found four of five Phase 1 options never produced a file. Late in the order because it wants T-108's ffmpeg rule settled first.

T-111, T-112 and T-114 are not in this list because nothing about their order is contentious: T-111 follows T-109, T-112 follows the spec, and T-114 can be taken by anyone at any point.

Four accepted entries have been amended to get here

The first two were proposed by the implementer and ratified separately

  • UX-005 — a finished playlist is one History row. Three sub-decisions: the chip counts, there is no segmented bar, and a partly-failed playlist says 14 items rather than 14 of 16, because a stored original count goes stale the first time a user removes a member.
  • DAT-005 §1 — reopened to admit clearing the whole list. It had refused Clear all and named the condition that would lift the refusal; T-125 met it. The verb is Clear history, because all has no object and the one a user supplies is their files.

Both were first recorded as maintainer rulings that had not happened, caught in review as T145-R1 and T144-R1. The content survived ratification unchanged; the attribution was the defect. Phase 3 has more of this ahead — T-113 reopens UX-001 by design.

The other two came with the History removal, on maintainer direction

  • REQ-020 is withdrawn outright — first narrowed to a private ledger by T-169, then withdrawn hours later. Nothing records what has been downloaded. REQ-021 reaches a file only while its queue row exists. REQ-022 is scoped to the live queue, where a duplicate is confirmed rather than refused: wanting the same URL twice is an ordinary thing to want.
  • DAT-001, DAT-005 and UX-005 carry appended amendments, never rewrites — AGENTS.md §6 makes those historical record. DAT-005's boundary is untouched: a record is not a file. UX-005 loses its second tab and keeps everything it decided about a row.
  • DAT-006 is Withdrawn, which is what T169-R1 was owed: it had been self-headed Accepted when the maintainer's direction established the product boundary and not the detailed data choices. The ruling that followed was that the ledger should not exist. Its reasoning against dropping columns survives it and still governs migration 0008's now-unused one.

The review found exactly that, and it was right. T169-R1: satisfying a decision's trigger makes it eligible for a ruling, not self-ratifying — the same distinction as T145-R1 and T144-R1, which this project has now recorded three times. Four further findings followed, and the maintainer's ruling was to remove the feature rather than repair it. Five findings, two High, and not one about the warning being wrong: every one was about keeping the data.
